Research

I am primarily interested in understanding stellar spectroscopic variability and its impact on radial velocity measurements, specifically the Rossiter-McLaughlin effect.

Radial Velocity Constraints of Europa's Opposition Surge during the 2026 January 10 Earth Transit

At opposition, Solar System objects with visible surfaces have a surge in brightness known as the opposition surge effect, which provides a unique opportunity to probe regolith and atmospheric properties. Here we present radial velocity (RV) observations of Europa, while the Earth transits the Sun from Europa's perspective, that are impacted by the opposition surge effect, resulting in a RV curve perturbation that is inverted, longer and orders of magnitudes larger than predicted by an RV model that only considers the portion of the Sun blocked by the Earth. We observed Europa with the NEID and Habitable Zone Planet Finder spectrographs surrounding the 2026 January 10 Earth transit as seen from Europa. To model the opposition surge effect on these RV observations, we develop the first RV model that integrates prescriptions for both physical mechanisms that can dominate the surge: shadow hiding (SH) and coherent backscattering (CB). We use the observed Europa RVs and our model to constrain Europa's opposition surge at near-zero phase angles and to test whether wavelength-dependent RVs can distinguish whether SH or CB dominates for Europa. Due to degeneracies in the obtained data, we are unable to definitively discriminate the dominant mechanism of Europa's opposition surge. Yet, this work establishes a modeling framework and demonstrates the potential of high-precision RVs during Solar System transits to characterize the opposition surge effect at near-zero phase angles.

Shadowing the Sun. I. Testing Synthetic Granulation Spectra with NEID Solar Eclipse Observations

The subtle Doppler radial velocity (RV) signature of an Earth-mass exoplanet in the Habitable Zone around a nearby Sun-like star is difficult to detect due to stellar variability. While multiple techniques aim to mitigate rotationally-modulated stellar activity, granulation, introducing 0.1-1 m/s RV shifts, currently sets the floor for detecting Earth-mass RV signals for low-activity stars. Observations of the Rossiter-McLaughlin (RM) effect during a solar eclipse, with a peak-to-trough amplitude of ~1.4 km/s, are a rigorous test of granulation and convective blueshift in the solar spectrum. Using NEID solar feed data of the October 2023 solar eclipse and GRASS-E (a geometry-modified version of GRASS), we compare the observed RVs for 22 lines with the simulated RVs from the synthetic granulation-driven spectra. Residuals of the GRASS-E Fe I 5434 Å model show an RMS of 13.47 m/s over the RM signal. We find that a model with an optimized convective blueshift polynomial for Fe I 5434 Å significantly outperforms GRASS-E with an RMS of only 2.90 m/s, suggesting that GRASS-E is currently limited by sparse center-to-limb observations. By combining a set of spectral lines, we achieve a weighted RMS of 5.2 m/s over the RM signal (~0.37%), a level of accuracy well beyond the reach of current exoplanet transit observations. In all, we demonstrate that solar eclipse observations allow us to recover the center-to-limb variation of the convective blueshift for individual spectral lines.
